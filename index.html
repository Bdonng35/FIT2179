THE UNEVEN MALAYSIA HOUSEHOLD REALITY

A visual story of income, poverty, inequality, basic living conditions, and expenditure across Malaysia.

Malaysia has grown richer over time, but growth has not translated into equal household wellbeing everywhere.

Income, inequality, expenditure and access to basic amenities reveal a country where development remains uneven across states.

About This Project

This interactive visualization examines regional disparities
across Malaysia, highlighting how aggregate national metrics
often hide underlying gaps in state-level development,
household wealth, and standard of living.

Highest Ranking States

Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya, and Selangor lead because high-value services, finance, administration, manufacturing, and logistics are concentrated in these metropolitan hubs.

Lowest Ranking States

Perak, Kedah, and Kelantan remain lower-income because their economies rely more on traditional agriculture and less on high-value industry, with weaker urbanization and slower infrastructure development.

Comparing income and expenditure reveals where higher earnings may come with greater cost pressures.

Note: States with higher household incomes often have larger gaps between their mean income and spending.

Regional Divergence

East Malaysia and rural peninsular states sit at the bottom, reflecting lower disposable household income and completely different consumption patterns.

Household spending patterns vary widely. Ranking states by average monthly expenditure shows where the absolute cost of living is highest.

High Spending States

Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya, and Selangor record the highest household expenditures, driven by high service costs and metropolitan lifestyles.

Budget Pressure

States like Kelantan and Pahang experience tighter budgets, where household spending consumes more than 70% of mean income.

Conclusion

Malaysia's household story is not simply one of economic growth. While the country has become richer, prosperity remains uneven, inequality persists, and basic amenities remain unequal. Real development requires focusing on household wellbeing, not just GDP.
